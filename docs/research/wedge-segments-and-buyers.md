# Wedge Segments and Buyers

**Date:** 2026-10-19  **Author:** market-research  **Question:** Which furnisher segment should be our first customer, who buys, and is the market big enough?
**Decision this informs:** ICP memo (first segment and buyer persona), pricing hypothesis, and whether the wedge is fundable on its own (U1, U4, U5).

## Bottom line
Target **mid-market nonbank consumer lenders first: fintech/online installment and card lenders and independent auto finance companies handling about 500-5,000 disputes a month (ACDV plus direct).** **Regional banks ($10B-$100B)** come second. Defer the top-10 issuers, which already run in-house and BPO operations at scale, and small credit unions, whose contract values are too small. Pricing hypothesis: a **platform fee of about $30-60K a year plus $3-5 per dispute**, giving a $60-150K ACV. Test it against an estimated loaded labor cost of about **$6-15 per dispute** (an assumption). Base serviceable market is **about $40M a year** (range $6M-$120M) and TAM is **about $180M** ($60M-$420M). That is enough for a wedge but probably not a venture-scale company by itself, so the expansion path (Metro 2 accuracy, collections, and the parked AI-audit layer) has to be part of the pitch.

**Confidence:** Low-medium. The structure of the market is A/B-grade, but volume per furnisher, willingness to pay, and the in-house share are assumptions. **We have no customer interviews.**
**What would change this answer:** discovery calls showing that fintechs see disputes as a cost center they won't pay to fix, or that regional banks have a funded remediation mandate (for example, after an MRA or a consent order). Either would flip the segment order. A current ACDV volume figure from e-OSCAR or CDIA would re-base the sizing.

## Key findings
1. **Volume is large and concentrated.** Evidence: A (dated). In 2011, consumers disputed 32-38M items and 85% were passed to furnishers. About 10,000 furnishers report monthly, and the top 10 supply 57% of tradelines (top 50: 72%). [S12]
2. **Pressure has risen sharply since then.** Evidence: B. Credit reporting complaints to the CFPB rose from about 70K a month (Jan 2023) to more than 460K a month (Oct 2025), about 86% of all complaints. Complaints are not disputes, so we use this only as a direction. [S8]
3. **Most furnisher dispute teams are small and manual.** Evidence: C/B (a vendor survey of 129 respondents, distributed with CDIA's help). 84% run disputes with 1-10 people, and 86% work ACDVs in the e-OSCAR web UI. Office tools are used by 77% for direct disputes (the PDF says 49% rely only on office tools). Unsecured lenders and servicers have the highest volume. 59% name "unclear reasonable investigation standards" as a challenge, and 33% say more than half their disputes are frivolous. Only 27-32% want more AI or automation now. The volume distribution is reported inconsistently (the press release says 53% handle 250 or fewer a month; the PDF chart says 7%), so treat it as directional. [S13]
4. **Large furnishers outsource to BPOs that already use AI tooling.** Evidence: B/C (vendor case study). HCLTech moved a "leading American" FI's credit bureau dispute and fraud operations to a vendor-run India and San Antonio model, using AI letter intake, both direct and indirect tracks, and a 10-15% AHT reduction. [S18] PSCU/TriVerity sells outsourced ACDV processing to credit unions (2022). [S19]
5. **The work is mostly in-house, low-wage, and runs on core systems plus spreadsheets.** Evidence: A (employer postings). TD Bank card disputes pays $23.25-30.75 an hour and uses e-OSCAR, TSYS/CAS, RCS/USBC, and Excel. FNBO (May 2026) pays $37-60K, uses e-OSCAR ACDV and AUD, and tracks direct disputes in "Direct Dispute logs and spreadsheets". Upstart has an "Operations Manager, Credit Bureau Disputes" posting (we saw only the title; the page did not load), which is a sign of an in-house team at a fintech. [S15][S16][S17]
6. **Collectors are high volume but low value.** Evidence: B (trade association survey, 2019). Collection agencies average 60,408 disputes a year (median 4,100), 77% "not generated by an individual", and about $10.7K a year in e-OSCAR fees. [S14] About 40% of disputes relate to collections. [S12]
7. **Regulatory heat is shifting toward banks.** Evidence: B. The CFPB's April 2025 memo moves supervision back toward large banks and depositories and away from nonbanks and fintechs, and names FCRA/Reg V data furnishing as a priority. [S6] This raises urgency for regional banks and lowers it for fintechs. Private FCRA litigation (on the ACDV channel) still applies to all furnishers (see the regulatory report).

**Buying roles (hypothesis, to test in discovery):** The **economic buyer** is the head of consumer lending operations or servicing, or the COO at a fintech; at banks, the business line owns the budget. The **champion** is the credit bureau disputes manager or director (the Upstart and FNBO-type role). **Users** are dispute specialists at $23-31 an hour [S15][S16]. **Blockers** are compliance and FCRA officers (who must defend "reasonable investigation"), third-party/vendor risk and InfoSec (PII going to an LLM, U7), model risk management at banks over $10B, and the e-OSCAR administrator, because OLDE must be notified of automation [primer log].

The SAM high (about 20M disputes) is above the top-down check: if the non-top-10 share is about 43% of roughly 40M, that is about 17M including collectors, so **the high case is likely too high**. **SOM (end of year 3):** 5 / 10 / 15 customers x $60K / $100K / $150K ACV = **$0.3M / $1.0M / $2.25M ARR**. At $4 a dispute, a credit union with 2,400 disputes is worth only about $10K a year, so **a platform-fee floor is required** or small furnishers are not worth selling to directly.

## Disconfirming evidence
- **"Large banks solved it in-house or through BPOs":** **partly confirmed.** BPOs already sell AI-assisted dispute operations to a large FI [S18], and the top 10 control 57% of tradelines [S12]. Over half the volume is probably unavailable to a startup in years 1-2.
- **"The market is too small":** **partly confirmed.** The base SAM is about $41M. Fundability depends on expansion beyond the wedge.
- **"Buyers don't want AI":** **partly confirmed.** Only 27% want more AI now, and 60% want technology to improve first [S13]. Positioning should lead with audit and defensibility, not autonomy.
- **"Regulatory retrenchment kills urgency":** **mixed.** CFPB exams have dropped from about 600 a year to about 70 expected in 2026 [S7], but furnishing remains a named priority [S6], and private litigation on the ACDV channel is unaffected.
- **"A dominant vendor exists":** **not found.** See competitive-landscape.md.

## Implications for product (to product-manager)
1. **ICP draft:** a US nonbank consumer lender (fintech unsecured loans or cards, or independent auto finance) with about 500-5,000 disputes a month (ACDV plus direct), an in-house team of 3-15, a named disputes manager, and recent litigation or audit findings. Regional banks are the second ICP.
2. Position the product as **"defensible investigations and audit record,"** with productivity as the secondary benefit (because of the low appetite for AI [S13] and the 59% citing an unclear reasonable investigation standard).
3. Build in a pricing floor (platform fee) from day one, and meter disputes.
4. Discovery must capture volume, FTEs, systems of record (U9), LLM hosting policy (U7), and who signed the last ops-tooling purchase.
